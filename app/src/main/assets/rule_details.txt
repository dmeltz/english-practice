@rule SP01
detail: Check the end of the base word. If it ends in one vowel followed by one consonant (stop, run, plan), and the stress falls on that last syllable, double the final consonant before a suffix that starts with a vowel: -ing, -ed, -er, -est.
detail: One-syllable words are always stressed, so they always double: sit → sitting, hot → hotter. Longer words double only if the last syllable is stressed: beGIN → beginning, preFER → preferred, but OPen → opening, VISit → visited, LISten → listening.
detail: Don't double after two vowels (rain → raining, cool → cooler) or after two consonants (help → helped, fast → faster). Never double w, x or y: show → showing, fix → fixed, play → played.
detail: British spelling also doubles a final l after an unstressed vowel (travel → travelled), American spelling doesn't (traveled). Both are accepted in the app.
good: We stopped at the gas station.
good: She is swimming in the pool.
good: It's getting hotter every year.
good: I preferred the first option.
good: The meeting is happening now.
good: They visited us last summer.
bad: He is runing late. => He is running late. | run = one vowel + one consonant, so double the n.
bad: We planed a trip. => We planned a trip. | planed is a different word (from "plane").
bad: The show is begining. => The show is beginning. | the stress is on -GIN, so double it.
bad: I am openning the door. => I am opening the door. | the stress is on O-pen, so no doubling.
bad: She helpped me. => She helped me. | help ends in two consonants, so no doubling.

@rule SP02
detail: Most English words that end in a silent e lose the e when you add a suffix that starts with a vowel (-ing, -ed, -er, -able, -ous, -ity): make → making, use → usable, nerve → nervous.
detail: Keep the e when the suffix starts with a consonant (-ly, -ful, -ment, -ness, -less): lovely, careful, movement, likeness, useless.
detail: A few exceptions: be → being, see → seeing, agree → agreeing (the e is pronounced), and argue → argument, true → truly, nine → ninth drop the e even before a consonant.
good: I'm writing an email.
good: She was hoping for good news.
good: The movement started in 2010.
good: That's a lovely idea.
good: We are taking the train.
good: Is this water usable?
bad: I am writeing a letter. => I am writing a letter. | -ing starts with a vowel, so drop the e.
bad: They are comming soon. => They are coming soon. | drop the e, and don't double the m.
bad: It was a lovly day. => It was a lovely day. | -ly starts with a consonant, so keep the e.
bad: I am hopping to see you. => I am hoping to see you. | hopping comes from hop (jump). hope → hoping.
bad: He was very carful. => He was very careful. | keep the e before -ful.

@rule SP03
detail: The letters c and g have a soft sound (s, j) before e, i and y, and a hard sound (k, g) before a, o and u. That's why words like notice and change keep their e before -able and -ous: without it, the c or g would sit before a or o and sound hard.
detail: So: notice → noticeable, replace → replaceable, manage → manageable, change → changeable, courage → courageous, outrage → outrageous, advantage → advantageous.
detail: Before -ing or -ed the e still drops, because i and e keep the sound soft anyway: noticing, changed, managing.
good: There's a noticeable difference.
good: The workload is manageable.
good: That was a courageous decision.
good: The weather here is very changeable.
good: She's changing her job.
bad: The change is noticable. => The change is noticeable. | keep the e so c stays soft.
bad: He is very knowledgable. => He is very knowledgeable. | keep the e after g.
bad: It was a couragous act. => It was a courageous act. | keep the e before -ous.
bad: She is changeing her plan. => She is changing her plan. | before -ing the e drops as usual.

@rule SP04
detail: When a word ends in a consonant + y, change the y to i before most suffixes: -ed, -es, -er, -est, -ly, -ness, -ful. study → studied, studies; happy → happier, happily, happiness; beauty → beautiful.
detail: Keep the y before -ing, because English avoids two i's in a row: study → studying, cry → crying, carry → carrying.
detail: When a vowel comes before the y, keep it: play → played, enjoy → enjoys, key → keys. Exceptions: pay → paid, say → said, lay → laid, day → daily.
good: She studied all weekend.
good: The baby is crying again.
good: Paris is one of the busiest cities.
good: They lived happily ever after.
good: He played very well.
good: We enjoyed the trip.
bad: He studyed hard. => He studied hard. | consonant + y → i before -ed.
bad: She is studiing now. => She is studying now. | keep y before -ing.
bad: There are three citys. => There are three cities. | consonant + y → ies.
bad: They stayed in two hotels and plaied tennis. => They stayed in two hotels and played tennis. | vowel + y: keep the y.
bad: I payed the bill. => I paid the bill. | pay → paid is an exception.

@rule SP05
detail: Words that end in a hissing sound (s, ss, sh, ch, x, z) need an extra syllable for the plural or the he/she/it form, so you add -es instead of -s: bus → buses, class → classes, dish → dishes, watch → watches, box → boxes, quiz → quizzes.
detail: The same rule works for verbs: she watches, he fixes, it crashes, my mother washes, the teacher teaches.
detail: If ch sounds like k, just add -s: stomach → stomachs, monarch → monarchs.
good: There are three buses an hour.
good: She watches the news every night.
good: He fixes old radios.
good: Put the glasses in the boxes.
good: My father wishes he had more time.
bad: She watchs TV. => She watches TV. | add -es after ch.
bad: We need more boxs. => We need more boxes. | add -es after x.
bad: He washs the car on Fridays. => He washes the car on Fridays. | add -es after sh.
bad: Two bus stopped. => Two buses stopped. | the plural needs -es.

@rule SP06
detail: Many common nouns that end in f or fe change it to v and add -es in the plural: knife → knives, life → lives, wife → wives, leaf → leaves, half → halves, shelf → shelves, wolf → wolves, thief → thieves, loaf → loaves. Also: self → selves, as in themselves, ourselves.
detail: Other words simply add -s: roof → roofs, chief → chiefs, belief → beliefs, cliff → cliffs, chef → chefs, proof → proofs. When in doubt, words ending in ff and words borrowed more recently usually just add -s.
detail: A few take either: scarf → scarves / scarfs, dwarf → dwarves / dwarfs.
good: Put the knives in the drawer.
good: The leaves are turning yellow.
good: They saved many lives.
good: All the roofs were damaged.
good: They did it themselves.
bad: The knifes are sharp. => The knives are sharp. | knife → knives.
bad: The books are on the shelfs. => The books are on the shelves. | shelf → shelves.
bad: Cut it into two halfs. => Cut it into two halves. | half → halves.
bad: The rooves are red. => The roofs are red. | roof just adds -s.

@rule SP07
detail: Most nouns that end in o just add -s, especially short words, words from other languages, and words with a vowel before the o: photos, pianos, radios, videos, kilos, zoos, kangaroos.
detail: A small group of common words take -es. Learn these: potatoes, tomatoes, heroes, echoes, vetoes, torpedoes. A few take either: mosquitoes / mosquitos, volcanoes / volcanos, zeroes / zeros.
detail: The verbs go and do also add -es: he goes, she does.
good: I bought potatoes and tomatoes.
good: Firefighters are real heroes.
good: Send me the photos.
good: He goes to the gym every day.
good: She does the shopping on Sundays.
bad: Add two tomatos. => Add two tomatoes. | tomato is one of the -es words.
bad: I took a lot of photoes. => I took a lot of photos. | photo just adds -s.
bad: He gos to work by bus. => He goes to work by bus. | go → goes.
bad: She dos her homework. => She does her homework. | do → does.

@rule SP08
detail: The old rhyme is "i before e, except after c, or when sounding like A, as in neighbour and weigh". It works for many common words: believe, piece, field, chief, friend; and after c: receive, ceiling, receipt, deceive, perceive.
detail: When the letters sound like "ay", use ei: eight, weigh, neighbour, vein, reign.
detail: The rule has exceptions that must simply be memorized: their, weird, height, foreign, either, neither, leisure, seize, protein. And after c: science, ancient, species, efficient (here the c sounds like sh or s plus a separate i).
good: I believe you.
good: Keep the receipt.
good: My neighbour is a doctor.
good: That's a weird idea.
good: What's your height?
good: She speaks a foreign language.
bad: I recieved your email. => I received your email. | after c, ei.
bad: Can I have a peice of cake? => Can I have a piece of cake? | ie, as usual.
bad: He is my best freind. => He is my best friend. | ie.
bad: Thier car is new. => Their car is new. | their is an exception.
bad: It's a wierd film. => It's a weird film. | weird is an exception.

@rule SP09
detail: When full becomes a suffix it loses one l: care + full → careful, help → helpful, beauty → beautiful, hope → hopeful, success → successful.
detail: To make an adverb, add -ly to -ful, which gives -fully with two l's: carefully, beautifully, hopefully, successfully.
detail: The same "one l" idea appears in until and welcome (not untill, wellcome), while the word full itself, standing alone, keeps both.
good: Be careful with that knife.
good: She drives carefully.
good: What a beautiful view!
good: The project was successful.
good: Hopefully it won't rain.
bad: Be carefull! => Be careful! | the suffix is -ful, one l.
bad: She sings beautifuly. => She sings beautifully. | -ful + -ly = -fully.
bad: It was a usefull meeting. => It was a useful meeting. | one l.
bad: We finished successfuly. => We finished successfully. | two l's in -fully.

@rule SP10
detail: Most adverbs are the adjective + -ly: quick → quickly, slow → slowly, careful → carefully. Words ending in e usually keep it: complete → completely, nice → nicely, extreme → extremely.
detail: Adjectives ending in -le drop the e and add y: simple → simply, possible → possibly, gentle → gently, terrible → terribly.
detail: Adjectives ending in -ic add -ally: basic → basically, automatic → automatically, dramatic → dramatically (exception: public → publicly). Words ending in consonant + y change y to i: happy → happily, easy → easily. And a few drop the e: true → truly, due → duly, whole → wholly.
good: I completely forgot.
good: He simply refused.
good: Basically, it's the same thing.
good: She smiled happily.
good: I truly appreciate it.
good: The door opens automatically.
bad: Basicly, yes. => Basically, yes. | -ic → -ically.
bad: I truely love it. => I truly love it. | true → truly is an exception.
bad: He spoke very gentlely. => He spoke very gently. | -le → -ly.
bad: I completly agree. => I completely agree. | keep the e.
bad: She won easyly. => She won easily. | y → i.

@rule SP11
detail: When all is joined to the front of a word, it keeps only one l: already, always, also, although, altogether, almost, almighty.
detail: Some phrases stay as two words: all right (alright is informal and not accepted in careful writing), a lot (never alot), all ready (= completely ready) vs already (= before now), all together (= everyone at once) vs altogether (= in total, completely).
detail: Also remember: in fact, at least, each other, in front, as well, and no one are all two words.
good: I've already eaten.
good: She always arrives early.
good: Thanks a lot!
good: Is everything all right?
good: We're all ready to go.
good: Although it was late, we stayed.
bad: I have allready finished. => I have already finished. | one l.
bad: He is allways late. => He is always late. | one l.
bad: I like it alot. => I like it a lot. | a lot is two words.
bad: Everything is allright. => Everything is all right. | two words, two l's.

@rule SP12
detail: These words are hard because you can't hear double letters. The best method is a picture or a phrase that fixes the count in your memory.
detail: necessary: one Collar, two Sleeves (1 c, 2 s). accommodate: big enough for two c's and two m's. embarrass: two r's and two s's, like going red twice. recommend: one c, two m's (re + commend). address: two d's, two s's. tomorrow: one m, two r's.
detail: Also: occasion (2 c, 1 s), committee (2 m, 2 t, 2 e), occurrence (2 c, 2 r), millennium (2 l, 2 n), possession (2 s twice), professional (1 f), disappoint (1 s, 2 p), beginning (2 n).
good: Is that really necessary?
good: I recommend the fish.
good: What's your email address?
good: See you tomorrow.
good: That was embarrassing.
good: Accommodation is expensive here.
bad: It's not neccessary. => It's not necessary. | one c, two s's.
bad: I recomend this book. => I recommend this book. | one c, two m's.
bad: See you tommorow. => See you tomorrow. | one m, two r's.
bad: I was so embarassed. => I was so embarrassed. | two r's, two s's.
bad: Write your adress here. => Write your address here. | two d's.

@rule SP13
detail: In English, vowels in unstressed syllables are reduced to a weak sound like "uh" (called schwa). So you can't hear whether separate has an a or an e in the middle. Spelling these needs a trick.
detail: Use a related word where the vowel is stressed: definite → definition (de-fi-NI-tion shows the i), separate → "there's a rat in separate", grammar → grammatical (the a is clear), independent → independence.
detail: Common -ar / -er / -or traps: grammar, calendar, dollar, but writer, teacher, and doctor, actor, visitor. Common -ent / -ant traps: independent, different, excellent vs important, relevant, pleasant.
good: I'll definitely be there.
good: Keep them in separate folders.
good: Check the calendar.
good: English grammar isn't easy.
good: She's very independent.
good: We had an argument.
bad: I will definately call. => I will definitely call. | think "finite".
bad: We sleep in seperate rooms. => We sleep in separate rooms. | "a rat" in separate.
bad: My grammer is improving. => My grammar is improving. | think "grammatical".
bad: Look at the calender. => Look at the calendar. | -ar.
bad: She is very independant. => She is very independent. | think "independence".

@rule SP14
detail: Many English words keep letters that were pronounced hundreds of years ago but are silent today. Learn them in groups by the pattern.
detail: kn- (k silent): know, knife, knee, knock, knowledge. wr- (w silent): write, wrong, wrap, wrist. -mb (b silent): climb, thumb, bomb, comb. -bt (b silent): doubt, debt. gn- / -gn (g silent): sign, foreign, design, gnome.
detail: Other common ones: island (s), listen and castle (t), Wednesday (first d), receipt (p), honest and hour (h), answer (w), half and talk (l), autumn (n).
good: I don't know.
good: Write your name here.
good: They live on an island.
good: Listen carefully.
good: See you on Wednesday.
good: Let me check the receipt.
bad: I don't no. => I don't know. | silent k.
bad: Rite it down. => Write it down. | silent w.
bad: I live on an iland. => I live on an island. | silent s.
bad: Lisen to me. => Listen to me. | silent t.
bad: We have a meeting on Wensday. => We have a meeting on Wednesday. | silent d.

@rule SP15
detail: These words sound the same (or almost) but mean different things. Spell-checkers won't catch them, so test each one by replacing it.
detail: their / there / they're: if you can say "they are", write they're. If it's "belonging to them", write their. Otherwise (place, or "there is / are") write there. your / you're: if "you are" fits, write you're. its / it's: if "it is" or "it has" fits, write it's. Otherwise its (belonging to it, no apostrophe, like his).
detail: to / too / two: too means also or more than enough (too hot), two is the number, everything else is to. then / than: than compares (bigger than), then is about time (and then). lose / loose: lose = not win, misplace (one o). loose = not tight (two o's).
good: They're happy with their new house over there.
good: You're right about your car.
good: It's raining. The cat is licking its paw.
good: It's too late to go to the two shops.
good: She is older than me. Then she left.
good: Don't lose your keys.
bad: Their coming tomorrow. => They're coming tomorrow. | they are → they're.
bad: Your welcome. => You're welcome. | you are → you're.
bad: The dog wagged it's tail. => The dog wagged its tail. | belongs to it → its.
bad: It's to expensive. => It's too expensive. | more than enough → too.
bad: He is taller then me. => He is taller than me. | comparing → than.
bad: Don't loose your phone. => Don't lose your phone. | lose = misplace.

@rule SP16
detail: Adding a prefix never changes the spelling of the word. Write the prefix, then the whole word, even if that creates a double letter: un + necessary = unnecessary, mis + spell = misspell, dis + satisfied = dissatisfied, im + mature = immature, il + legal = illegal, ir + regular = irregular.
detail: And if there's no double letter, don't invent one: dis + appear = disappear (one s), dis + agree = disagree, mis + take = mistake.
detail: in- changes to im- before b, m, p (impossible, immature), il- before l (illegal), ir- before r (irregular). That's where the doubled letters come from.
good: That's unnecessary.
good: I misspelled his name.
good: The keys disappeared.
good: She was dissatisfied with the service.
good: It's illegal to park here.
good: Go is an irregular verb.
bad: It's unecessary. => It's unnecessary. | un + necessary = two n's.
bad: Don't mispell it. => Don't misspell it. | mis + spell = two s's.
bad: He dissappeared. => He disappeared. | dis + appear = one s.
bad: That's ilegal. => That's illegal. | il + legal = two l's.

@rule GR01
detail: In the present simple, the verb takes -s (or -es) with he, she, it, and with any single person or thing (my brother, the train, everybody). With I, you, we and they there's no -s.
detail: Spelling follows the plural rules: watches, fixes (SP05), studies (SP04), goes, does (SP07). Irregular: have → has.
detail: In questions and negatives the -s moves to does, and the main verb loses it: She works → Does she work? → She doesn't work (see GR03).
good: She works in Tel Aviv.
good: My son has a new bike.
good: Everybody knows that.
good: The train leaves at 7.
good: It costs 20 shekels.
good: They live near the beach.
bad: He work in a bank. => He works in a bank. | he → add -s.
bad: She have two cats. => She has two cats. | have → has.
bad: My mother cook very well. => My mother cooks very well. | one person → -s.
bad: Everybody like him. => Everybody likes him. | everybody is singular.
bad: They lives in Haifa. => They live in Haifa. | they → no -s.

@rule GR02
detail: Hebrew sentences like הוא עייף or איפה התחנה have no verb in the present. English sentences always need one, so add am, is or are: He is tired. Where is the station?
detail: I am, he/she/it is, you/we/they are. In speech and informal writing these are usually shortened: I'm, he's, it's, you're, we're, they're.
detail: This also applies before -ing (I am working, see GR04) and in questions: Is she here? Are you ready? Where are my keys?
good: He is a doctor.
good: The kids are at school.
good: Where is the bathroom?
good: I'm not ready yet.
good: Are you hungry?
good: My phone is on the table.
bad: He a doctor. => He is a doctor. | needs is.
bad: The children in the garden. => The children are in the garden. | needs are.
bad: Where the station? => Where is the station? | needs is.
bad: I very tired. => I am very tired. | needs am.
bad: You hungry? => Are you hungry? | the question starts with are.

@rule GR03
detail: In the present simple, questions and negatives need a helper: do (I, you, we, they) or does (he, she, it). The main verb then stays in its base form, without -s.
detail: Questions: (Question word) + do/does + subject + verb: Do you like it? Where does she work? What do they want?
detail: Negatives: don't / doesn't + verb: I don't know. He doesn't eat meat. Exception: the verb be doesn't use do: Are you ready? She isn't here. Also can, will, must and similar verbs: Can you swim? I can't.
good: Do you speak English?
good: Where does he live?
good: She doesn't like coffee.
good: We don't have time.
good: What time does the shop open?
good: Are you tired?
bad: You like pizza? => Do you like pizza? | a question needs do.
bad: Where he works? => Where does he work? | does + base verb.
bad: She don't know. => She doesn't know. | she → doesn't.
bad: He doesn't likes it. => He doesn't like it. | after doesn't, no -s.
bad: Do you are ready? => Are you ready? | be doesn't take do.

@rule GR04
detail: Use the present simple for habits, routines and facts: I work from home. She usually drinks tea. Water boils at 100 degrees.
detail: Use the present continuous (am/is/are + -ing) for what's happening right now or around now: I am working now. She is drinking coffee today. It's raining.
detail: Hebrew has only one present form, so the choice must be made in English. State verbs normally don't take -ing even "now": know, understand, believe, want, need, like, love, hate, prefer, mean, own, belong, seem, and have when it means own. Say: I know, I want, I have a car (not "I am knowing / wanting / having a car").
good: I work in an office.
good: I'm working from home today.
good: She usually walks to work.
good: Look! It's snowing.
good: I understand you now.
good: He needs help.
bad: I am working here since 2010. => I have worked here since 2010. | from a time until now → present perfect (GR07).
bad: I am knowing the answer. => I know the answer. | know doesn't take -ing.
bad: Look, the baby sleeps. => Look, the baby is sleeping. | right now → -ing.
bad: Every day I am going to the gym. => Every day I go to the gym. | a habit → present simple.
bad: I am wanting a coffee. => I want a coffee. | want doesn't take -ing.

@rule GR05
detail: In past simple questions and negatives, the helper did shows that it's past. The main verb goes back to its base form: Did you go? I didn't go. (Not "did you went", "I didn't went".)
detail: The pattern: (Question word) + did + subject + base verb: Did you see it? What did she say? Where did they go? Negative: didn't + base verb: He didn't call.
detail: Just like GR03, the verb be doesn't use did: Were you there? I wasn't ready.
good: Did you see the match?
good: I didn't sleep well.
good: What did he say?
good: Where did you buy it?
good: She didn't come yesterday.
good: Were you at home?
bad: I didn't went there. => I didn't go there. | didn't + base verb.
bad: Did you saw it? => Did you see it? | did + base verb.
bad: What did she said? => What did she say? | did + base verb.
bad: He didn't knew. => He didn't know. | didn't + base verb.
bad: Did you were there? => Were you there? | be doesn't take did.

@rule GR06
detail: Regular verbs add -ed in the past (worked, played), but many of the most common verbs are irregular. Their past forms must be learned, and some have tricky spellings.
detail: The -ought / -aught group: buy → bought, bring → brought, think → thought, fight → fought, seek → sought; catch → caught, teach → taught. (Both groups sound the same: "ot".)
detail: Other common ones: go → went, come → came, see → saw, say → said, pay → paid, make → made, take → took, get → got, give → gave, leave → left, lose → lost, choose → chose, write → wrote, read → read (pronounced "red"), know → knew, feel → felt, keep → kept, sleep → slept, meet → met, sit → sat.
good: I bought a new phone.
good: She brought cake to work.
good: I thought you knew.
good: He taught me to drive.
good: We went home early.
good: They chose the red one.
bad: I buyed it yesterday. => I bought it yesterday. | buy → bought.
bad: She brang her dog. => She brought her dog. | bring → brought.
bad: I thinked about it. => I thought about it. | think → thought.
bad: He catched the ball. => He caught the ball. | catch → caught.
bad: She teached English. => She taught English. | teach → taught.
bad: I payed in cash. => I paid in cash. | pay → paid.

@rule GR07
detail: Hebrew uses the present for something that started in the past and is still going on: אני גר פה עשר שנים. English uses the present perfect: I have lived here for ten years. The present simple ("I live here for ten years") is wrong here.
detail: Form: have/has + past participle (lived, worked, known, been). For actions in progress you can also use have/has been + -ing: I have been waiting for an hour.
detail: for + a length of time: for ten years, for an hour, for a long time. since + the starting point: since 2016, since Monday, since I was a child. Questions: How long have you known him?
good: I've lived here for ten years.
good: She has worked here since 2020.
good: How long have you known him?
good: We've been married for five years.
good: I've been waiting since 9 o'clock.
good: He hasn't called since Monday.
bad: I live here for ten years. => I have lived here for ten years. | started in the past, still true → present perfect.
bad: I know him since 2015. => I have known him since 2015. | present perfect with since.
bad: How long do you work here? => How long have you worked here? | how long until now → present perfect.
bad: I have lived here since five years. => I have lived here for five years. | a length of time → for.
bad: She is waiting for an hour. => She has been waiting for an hour. | present perfect continuous.

@rule GR08
detail: Both tenses talk about the past, but the past simple is tied to a finished time, and the present perfect isn't. If you say or mean when it happened (yesterday, last year, in 2010, two days ago, when I was young), use the past simple: I saw him yesterday.
detail: Use the present perfect when the time isn't said or doesn't matter, for life experience, and with ever, never, already, yet, just, so far: Have you ever been to Japan? I've never tried sushi. Have you finished yet? He's just arrived.
detail: Questions with when always take the past simple: When did you arrive? (Not "When have you arrived?") American English often uses the past simple with already, yet and just too (Did you eat yet?), and the app accepts that where it's natural.
good: I went to London last year.
good: I've been to London twice.
good: Have you ever eaten sushi?
good: She moved here two months ago.
good: I haven't finished yet.
good: When did you start?
bad: I have seen him yesterday. => I saw him yesterday. | yesterday is a finished time.
bad: Did you ever go to Italy? => Have you ever been to Italy? | life experience → present perfect.
bad: When have you arrived? => When did you arrive? | when → past simple.
bad: I didn't eat yet. => I haven't eaten yet. | yet → present perfect (in British English).
bad: We have met in 2015. => We met in 2015. | in 2015 is a finished time.

@rule GR09
detail: Hebrew has no word for a/an, so it's easy to drop it. In English a single countable noun (one car, one job, one idea) can't stand alone: it needs a, an, the, or a word like my, this, one.
detail: This includes jobs: I am an engineer. She is a teacher. And descriptions: It's a good idea. He's a nice guy. What a beautiful day!
detail: Choose a or an by the first sound, not the first letter: an apple, an hour, an honest man, an MBA (em-bee-ay), but a university (yoo), a European (yoo), a one-way ticket (wun). Plural and uncountable nouns don't take a/an: I need advice (not "an advice"), They are engineers.
good: I'm an engineer.
good: She has a car and a dog.
good: It took an hour.
good: He studies at a university.
good: That's a good idea.
good: They are teachers.
bad: I am doctor. => I am a doctor. | a job needs a/an.
bad: It is good idea. => It is a good idea. | one idea → a.
bad: We waited a hour. => We waited an hour. | hour starts with a vowel sound.
bad: She goes to an university. => She goes to a university. | university starts with a "yoo" sound.
bad: He gave me an advice. => He gave me advice. | advice is uncountable (GR11).

@rule GR10
detail: When you talk about things in general, use plural or uncountable nouns with no article: Dogs are loyal. I love music. Life is short. Money isn't everything. Hebrew often uses ה here (החיים קצרים), English doesn't.
detail: Use the when you mean specific things that both of you can identify: The dogs next door are noisy. The music at the party was great. Pass me the salt.
detail: Also no article with most meals, sports, school subjects and languages when speaking generally: I play tennis. She studies history. We had lunch. He speaks Hebrew. But: the piano, the guitar (playing instruments), the sea, the sun, the internet.
good: Life is beautiful.
good: I don't eat meat.
good: Cats sleep a lot.
good: The people here are friendly.
good: Time is money.
good: She plays the piano.
bad: The life is short. => Life is short. | life in general → no article.
bad: I like the music. => I like music. | music in general → no article (unless you mean specific music).
bad: The children need sleep. => Children need sleep. | children in general → no article.
bad: I play the football. => I play football. | sports → no article.
bad: Pass me salt. => Pass me the salt. | the specific salt on the table → the.

@rule GR11
detail: Some nouns are uncountable in English even though they're countable in Hebrew. They have no plural, don't take a/an, and use a singular verb: advice, information, news, furniture, luggage, baggage, equipment, homework, work (= labour), research, knowledge, progress, traffic, weather, money, bread, and most liquids and materials.
detail: To count them use a piece of / an item of / a bit of: a piece of advice, two pieces of furniture, an item of news.
detail: Uncountable: much, little, a little, a lot of. Countable: many, few, a few, a lot of. How much money? How many people? Note: news is singular: The news is good.
good: She gave me some good advice.
good: I need more information.
good: The news is great.
good: How much luggage do you have?
good: How many people came?
good: We bought new furniture.
bad: He gave me advices. => He gave me advice. | advice has no plural.
bad: I need an information. => I need some information. | no a/an with uncountable.
bad: The news are bad. => The news is bad. | news is singular.
bad: How much people were there? => How many people were there? | people is countable → many.
bad: I have many homeworks. => I have a lot of homework. | homework is uncountable.

@rule GR12
detail: Time. at for a point: at 5 o'clock, at noon, at night, at the weekend (British). on for days and dates: on Monday, on 3 May, on my birthday, on the weekend (American). in for longer periods: in June, in 2020, in summer, in the morning, in the evening, in ten minutes (= after ten minutes).
detail: Place. at for a point or a usual place: at home, at work, at school, at the station, at the door. on for surfaces and lines, and for public transport: on the table, on the wall, on the 3rd floor, on the bus, on the train, on the phone, on TV. in for an enclosed space or area: in the room, in the car, in a taxi, in Tel Aviv, in Israel.
detail: Hebrew uses ב for most of these, so the English choice must be learned. No preposition before next / last / this / every: next Monday, last week, every day.
good: The meeting is at 3 o'clock.
good: See you on Sunday.
good: I was born in 1985.
good: She's at work.
good: I'm on the bus.
good: We'll meet next week.
bad: I am in work. => I am at work. | usual place → at.
bad: See you in Monday. => See you on Monday. | days → on.
bad: The meeting is in 5 o'clock. => The meeting is at 5 o'clock. | clock time → at.
bad: He is in the bus. => He is on the bus. | public transport → on.
bad: We met at 2019. => We met in 2019. | years → in.
bad: I'll call you on next week. => I'll call you next week. | no preposition before next.

@rule GR13
detail: Adjectives go before the noun in English (the opposite of Hebrew): a red car, a big house, new shoes. Several adjectives usually follow opinion → size → age → colour: a beautiful big old red car.
detail: Don't put anything between a verb and its object: I like it very much (not "I like very much it"). She speaks English well (not "She speaks well English"). Adverbs like always, usually, never go before the main verb but after be: She always drinks tea. She is always late.
detail: In an indirect question (inside another sentence), use normal statement order and no do: Do you know where he is? (not "where is he"). Can you tell me what time it is? I don't know what she wants.
good: I have a new phone.
good: I like it very much.
good: She speaks English very well.
good: Do you know where the station is?
good: I don't know what he wants.
good: He is always late.
bad: I have a car red. => I have a red car. | adjective before the noun.
bad: I like very much this song. => I like this song very much. | verb + object together.
bad: Do you know where is the bank? => Do you know where the bank is? | statement order inside a question.
bad: She speaks very well English. => She speaks English very well. | verb + object together.
bad: He goes always to the gym. => He always goes to the gym. | always before the main verb.

@rule GR14
detail: One-syllable adjectives add -er / -est: tall → taller → the tallest, cheap → cheaper → the cheapest. Spelling rules apply: big → bigger (SP01), nice → nicer (SP02), easy → easier (SP04). Two-syllable adjectives ending in y work the same: happy → happier, busy → busiest.
detail: Longer adjectives use more / the most: expensive → more expensive → the most expensive, interesting → more interesting. Never both: not "more taller", "more better", "the most biggest".
detail: Irregular: good → better → the best, bad → worse → the worst, far → further/farther → the furthest. Compare with than (not then): taller than me. Use the before the superlative: the best, the cheapest.
good: He is taller than his father.
good: This is the cheapest option.
good: This phone is more expensive.
good: Today is better than yesterday.
good: It's the worst film I've ever seen.
good: The test was easier than I thought.
bad: She is more taller than me. => She is taller than me. | -er or more, never both.
bad: It's more good. => It's better. | good → better.
bad: It's the most cheap. => It's the cheapest. | short adjective → -est.
bad: He is older then me. => He is older than me. | compare with than.
bad: This is more bad. => This is worse. | bad → worse.

@rule GR15
detail: After if, when, before, after, as soon as, until and unless, English uses the present to talk about the future. The other half of the sentence uses will (or an instruction). Hebrew uses the future in both parts, which leads to "If it will rain". Don't.
detail: If it rains, I will stay home. When I arrive, I'll call you. I'll wait until you come back. Before you leave, close the window. As soon as I know, I'll tell you.
detail: The two halves can swap places: I'll call you when I arrive. Use a comma when the if/when part comes first. The same applies to the present perfect for completed actions: I'll call you when I've finished.
good: If it rains, we'll stay inside.
good: Call me when you get home.
good: I'll wait until you come back.
good: As soon as I know, I'll tell you.
good: Before you go, close the door.
good: If you need help, ask me.
bad: If it will rain, I will stay home. => If it rains, I will stay home. | no will after if.
bad: When I will arrive, I'll call. => When I arrive, I'll call. | no will after when.
bad: I'll wait until you will come. => I'll wait until you come. | no will after until.
bad: If you will need something, tell me. => If you need something, tell me. | no will after if.

@rule GR16
detail: Offers and suggestions: Hebrew asks with an infinitive (לפתוח את החלון?), English uses Shall I...? or Should I...?: Shall I open the window? Should I call him? Also: Do you want me to...? Would you like me to...?
detail: make is for creating, producing or causing something: make a mistake, make a decision, make a reservation, make a plan, make money, make noise, make dinner, make friends, make a phone call.
detail: do is for activities, tasks and work in general: do homework, do the dishes, do the shopping, do sport, do exercise, do business, do a favour, do your best, do nothing. לעשות can be either one, so learn the pairs. להזמין is also tricky: invite people, order food, book or reserve a table.
good: Shall I close the door?
good: Should I bring anything?
good: I made a mistake.
good: We need to make a decision.
good: I'll do the dishes.
good: Can you do me a favour?
bad: To open the window? => Shall I open the window? | offers → Shall I / Should I.
bad: I did a mistake. => I made a mistake. | make a mistake.
bad: He makes his homework. => He does his homework. | do homework.
bad: I want to make an invitation for 8. => I want to make a reservation for 8. | a table → reservation / book.
bad: Let's do a decision. => Let's make a decision. | make a decision.
